### Combined Response

As a twenty-year-old Interactive Media major, I have always felt like I was relatively comfortable with technology. I use my laptop every day for school, communicate through social media and messaging apps, and work with different software for my classes and projects. However, I have started to realize that being digitally literate is not just about knowing how to use technology. It is also about understanding how to use it intentionally, knowing where information comes from, and being able to question the tools I am using. It isn't just about knowing how to use the tool as much as knowing how the tool works. As Drucker mentions in Chapter 1 of her book, [_The Digital Humanities Coursebook_](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781003106531-1/digital-humanities-overview-johanna-drucker?context=ubx&refId=d32fa15e-299e-4b0e-a0b7-496606198488), there is a lot of tools that have a blackbox around them, the processing side of things tends to be lost on us.


For web-based projects, I am most comfortable working with HTML, CSS, JavaScript, p5.js. I have gotten better at using Markdown and can work with YAML as well. I have little (and limited) experience with XML as well. Through _Introduction to Digital Humanities_ and _Computational Social Science_, I would consider myself well-adjusted to R. I often use GitHub to manage projects and share code, as well as collaborate with others on works. A lot of my work involves combining design and technology, so I am used to going back and forth between making something visually appealing and figuring out how to actually make it work. I think it is definitely a struggle to find the balance between interactiveness and functionality. For everyday communication, I use platforms like Instagram, WhatsApp, and Discord, although I think I have become more aware of how differently I communicate depending on the platform. I would not write something for a class Discord in the same way that I would post it on Instagram. These are small things, but they are also part of digital literacy.

I have worked with Python, R, spreadsheets and Jupyter notebooks to clean, analyse and visualise data. The project I learned the most from was one where we analysed 1,000 science fiction books in R to see whether the way words associated with war were used changed over time, and then tried to visualize what we found. I think that task is what made me much more comfortable with data analysis, our results weren't exactly what we expected but we still learned a lot. Additionally, I now know a range of methods, and I understand ideas like causality, confounders and mediators from my _Computational Social Science_ class. These matter because it is important to realize the associations your variables have with eachother. I also try to use Zotero as often as I can to keep track of my sources, although my habits for organising research are still developing. Being able to organize my files when researching (in some way it kind of becomes my very own dataset), is important because if I have a tone of sources but they aren't organized, it would be very time consuming to map each one to a a cohesive literature review, which is why in _Capstone Seminar_ I am happy that we were urged to make a source map. 

This class is one of the classes were I see my skills from different disciplines (which I always thought of as seperate and not connected) work together. While a lot of people are taking this class as an Interactive Media elective, we all bring our unique insights to class. For both assignments we've had so far, I've worked with an art history major and it was very interesting to see how what we both brought to the table. I am more familiar with the technical side of things, so I usually explain and help with the coding parts but my partner helps shed a different lense on our work (a more historical one). As cornt as it sounds, it's like I was the Digital to her Humanities. I think collaborating in a multidisciplinary space is something everyone should try at least once in their career, if it allows for it, as it is amazing to see how much depth a project can get based on the different scopes people from different fields can offer. 

That's what makes Digital Humanities so intriguing to me because it sits between these different ways of thinking. I used to think of the humanities and technology as fairly separate areas, but this course has shown me that things like literature, history, language, images, and culture can also become data that can be analyzed computationally. At the same time, the way that data is organized and presented can affect how people interpret it. This connects closely to Interactive Media because an interface is not neutral. The way information is presented can change what a person notices, what they understand, and even how they feel about it. As we go more into image data, I am brought back to Fei Fei Li and Computer Vision. In her [Ted Talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=40riCqvRoMs), she talks about ImageNet and the training of models. For my capstone, we are using a pre-trained model called YOLO and it can detect a lot of things and hearing Li talk about the foundation that she helped build to get us to where we are now on computer vision makes me in awe of her. I feel like with the rise of AI we tend to take technical advancements a bit more for granted, it's harder for the younger generations to conceptualize how much hard work it took to get technology to where it is today.

My understanding of AI has also become more nuanced. I use AI for things like explaining code, or helping me get unstuck on a bug, and I appreciate that this course helps us learn how to use AI in a more balanced manner. We learn to be better at prompting and understanding what tasks AI can automate, like giving it screenshots of tables and asking it to make it into a CSV. If I were to manually do that it would take such a long time. I also think a lot of people think AI and genAI are the same thing, not that genAI is a subset of AI, I tend to make that mistake sometimes and I think it's something worth noting.

Overall, I think this time around, I am writing/editing the response in the shoes of someone who has developed more skills, and experimented with types of data she wasn't familiar with (spatial and image). I used to think of data mainly as something in spreadsheet format, but as this course progresses, I think of it as maps, as photographs. I think of different ways I can visualize the data, or manipulate the image. I've broadened my scope, and I think it's made me more digitally literate than I was.
