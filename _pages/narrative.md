## 1. **Gemini:**

Navigating the Digital Ecosystem: An Interactive Media Perspective

As a twenty-year-old Interactive Media major, my understanding of digital literacy has undergone a massive evolution over the past couple of years. If you had asked me in high school what it meant to be digitally literate, I probably would have pointed to my ability to curate an aesthetic Instagram feed or troubleshoot a Wi-Fi router. Today, sitting in a university classroom surrounded by peers from Computer Science, Film and New Media, and Creative Writing, I realize that digital literacy is far more than operational competence. It is a fundamental fluency in the languages, tools, and ethics of our modern information ecosystem.

In my daily life, my digital toolkit is a hybrid of social coordination, and academic research. For social interaction I rely heavily on Discord and WhatsApp to mobilize peers, and Canva to design shareable graphics for social media. Academically, my research data management currently leans on Zotero to organize an endless stream of PDFs and literature reviews. When it comes to data manipulation and analysis, I am comfortable with Excel and Google Sheets for basic statistical work, and I would like to start dipping my toes into QGIS for spatial mapping and R for parsing ecological datasets. Yet, despite knowing my way around these platforms, I often feel like I am just scratching the surface of what digital tools can actually do.

This feeling of being a novice is amplified in an interdisciplinary classroom. It is both intimidating and deeply inspiring to look around the room and see the varied skill sets my peers possess. The Computer Science and Engineering majors inherently understand the architecture of the software I merely consume; they speak in Python and Java, viewing problems as algorithms waiting to be optimized. Meanwhile, the Interactive Media and Film majors understand how to design immersive digital experiences that evoke emotional responses, and the Literature majors excel at dissecting the narratives and biases embedded within digital texts. 

This is exactly where the Digital Humanities (DH) are starting to offer entirely new horizons for my studies. Before this course, I viewed the humanities and the hard sciences as parallel tracks that rarely crossed. Now, I see DH as the bridge. For instance, applying digital humanities methodologies to environmental history—like using text-mining algorithms to analyze centuries of agricultural records, or building interactive, historical maps of changing coastlines—allows us to humanize data. It teaches me that ecological data does not exist in a vacuum; it is deeply entangled with human history, policy, and narrative.

Collaborating with peers across these diverse majors is proving to be the most effective way to develop my "computational thinking." When I sit down with a CS major to discuss tracking urban heat islands, they force me to break down a massive, abstract environmental issue into logical, sequential steps that a computer can process. They teach me how to structure queries and think about data architecture. In return, I find myself helping them contextualize their code, reminding them of the socio-economic factors that explain why certain neighborhoods lack tree canopies. This collaborative friction is where the real learning happens.

Furthermore, this interdisciplinary exposure is fundamentally shifting how I approach data and the looming world of AI. AI used to sound to me like an esoteric concept reserved for tech hubs, or a threat to academic integrity. Now, I am beginning to view machine learning models as powerful allies in climate modeling and predictive ecology. However, I am also learning to approach AI critically. Data is not inherently objective; algorithms can inherit the biases of their human creators. If we use AI to model future climate refugees, we must ask whose data is training the model and who is being left out of the equation.

Reflecting on my current abilities, I recognize a lingering hesitation to dive deep into raw coding. I am often afraid of breaking things or hitting a wall of syntax errors. Yet, what I am discovering in this course is that I do not need to become a master programmer to be highly digitally literate. Instead, I need to know how to ask the right questions of the technology, how to collaborate with those who build it, and how to critically evaluate its output. Moving forward, the new way of thinking I most want to explore is digital storytelling through data visualization. I want to learn how to take cold, hard climate data and, using computational tools and humanistic insights, transform it into narratives that compel people to act. Digital literacy, I am realizing, is ultimately about translation—translating code into knowledge, and data into meaningful change.

## 2. **Claude:**
## Reflection: Digital Literacy

*Interactive Media major, Environmental Studies minor — Year 3*

I came into this course assuming I was already digitally literate, mostly because I have never known a version of my life without a screen in it. Three weeks in, I think what I actually had was fluency with interfaces. I know how to *use* things. I am much less sure I know how they work, who built them, what they do with what I give them, or what they quietly make easy and hard.

My content creation habits are the part I feel most confident about. I build in Figma when I'm sketching an interaction, move into React or p5.js when I want the thing to actually run, and use Premiere and DaVinci when the project is time-based. I've learned a few small disciplines that turn out to matter more than software knowledge: keeping assets in a predictable folder structure, committing to Git early instead of after everything breaks, writing a README for my future self, and keeping a changelog of what I tried and abandoned. Those aren't glamorous skills, but they're the ones that let me hand a project to someone else. On the social side I've become fairly deliberate about platform register — a process post on Instagram, a technical writeup on a personal site, a thread in our class Discord for the half-formed question I don't want to email a professor about. I've also gotten better at reading a comment section as data rather than as a verdict.

Where I feel thin is research data management. For my Environmental Studies work I've been handed sensor datasets and survey exports, and my instinct has always been to open them in Excel and start clicking. I'm learning that this is a bad instinct — not because Excel is bad, but because clicking leaves no trace. Pandas in a Jupyter notebook does the same work but produces a record of the reasoning. I've started using Zotero properly instead of a bookmarks folder, OpenRefine for the cleaning stage I used to do by hand, and I'm trying to internalize the idea that raw data should stay raw and every transformation should live in a script. For visualization I move between matplotlib for exploratory work, Observable or D3 when the output is meant to be read by someone else, and QGIS when the question is spatial, which in my minor it usually is.

The most genuinely new thing for me is digital humanities, mostly because it reframes what counts as a dataset. I had been treating "data" as something numeric that arrives from an instrument. Seeing text corpora, archival photographs, oral histories, and map layers treated as computable objects has been disorienting in a useful way. For Interactive Media it suggests that an interface can be an argument — that how a user moves through an archive is itself an interpretive claim. For my environmental work it suggests that the flood data and the testimony of people who lived through the flood belong in the same analytical frame, and that keeping them separate was a choice I hadn't noticed making.

Sitting in a room with computer science, film, engineering, and literature students has been the actual curriculum. The CS students think in terms of decomposition and edge cases; the literature students ask what a model is *claiming*; the film students have a vocabulary for pacing and attention that I don't have. Computational thinking, as far as I can tell, isn't a programming skill — it's the habit of breaking a problem into pieces, finding the pattern, abstracting away the noise, and writing down the procedure clearly enough that someone else could follow it. I've learned more of that from pair-debugging with an engineering student and from a Creative Writing student asking "but what is the unit of analysis here?" than from any tutorial.

On AI I'm somewhere between dependent and skeptical. I use it to scaffold code and unstick myself, and it's genuinely good at that. But I've caught myself accepting a plausible-looking function I couldn't have written and wouldn't have caught if it were wrong, which is a real erosion of something. I'd like to move from using these tools as answer machines toward understanding them as statistical objects with training data, assumptions, and failure modes — and to ask the environmental question about them too, since the compute has a footprint.

What I want to develop next: reproducible workflows I actually maintain, enough statistics to know when I'm fooling myself, and the critical vocabulary to argue about infrastructure rather than just features. My literacy right now is wide and shallow. I'd like to trade some of that width for depth.


## 3.**ChatGPT**

### Reflection: Digital Literacy

As a twenty-year-old Interactive Media major, I have always felt like I was relatively comfortable with technology. I use my laptop every day for school, communicate through social media and messaging apps, and work with different software for my classes and projects. However, I have started to realize that being digitally literate is not just about knowing how to use technology. It is also about understanding how to use it intentionally, knowing where information comes from, and being able to question the tools I am using.

For content creation, I am most comfortable working with HTML, CSS, JavaScript, p5.js, and other tools that allow me to build interactive experiences. I have also used GitHub to manage projects and share code. A lot of my work involves combining design and technology, so I am used to going back and forth between making something visually appealing and figuring out how to actually make it work. For everyday communication, I use platforms like Instagram, WhatsApp, and Discord, although I think I have become more aware of how differently I communicate depending on the platform. I would not write something for a class Discord in the same way that I would post it on Instagram. These are small things, but they are also part of digital literacy.

When it comes to research and data, I still have more to learn. I have worked with Python, spreadsheets, and datasets, and I have used tools like Jupyter notebooks for manipulating and visualizing data. I understand basic ideas like cleaning data, comparing variables, and creating graphs, but I am not always confident when I have to work with a dataset that is unfamiliar to me. I also use tools like Zotero to keep track of sources, although I am still developing better habits for organizing research. One thing I am learning is that managing information is just as important as finding it. Having a large number of sources or a large dataset does not automatically make the research better.

The interdisciplinary nature of this course has made me think about these skills differently. Being surrounded by students studying Computer Science, Engineering, Film and New Media, Literature, Creative Writing, and Environmental Studies means that everyone approaches digital work from a different starting point. A Computer Science student might immediately think about how to build a system, while someone studying Literature might focus more on the language and meaning within the same system. Someone from Film might think about how an audience experiences it visually. As an Interactive Media student, I tend to think about what the user will actually do and experience. None of these perspectives completely replaces the others, which is what makes collaboration useful.

Digital Humanities is especially interesting to me because it sits between these different ways of thinking. I used to think of the humanities and technology as fairly separate areas, but this course has shown me that things like literature, history, language, images, and culture can also become data that can be analyzed computationally. At the same time, the way that data is organized and presented can affect how people interpret it. This connects closely to Interactive Media because an interface is not neutral. The way information is presented can change what a person notices, what they understand, and even how they feel about it.

Working with people from different backgrounds also helps develop computational thinking. I used to associate computational thinking mostly with programming, but I now see it more as a way of breaking a complicated problem into smaller parts and figuring out how those parts relate to each other. I can learn this from someone who knows more programming than I do, while they might learn from me how to think about design, interaction, or the experience of the person using what we create.

My understanding of AI has also become more complicated. I use AI for things like brainstorming, explaining code, or helping me get unstuck, but I have become more aware that getting an answer is not the same as understanding something. AI can produce something that looks convincing while still being inaccurate or oversimplified. This means that digital literacy also involves knowing when to question an output and verify information independently. I want to become better at using AI as a tool without letting it replace my own thinking.

Overall, I think my digital literacy is strongest when I am creating and experimenting, but I still have a lot to develop when it comes to data, research methods, and understanding the systems behind the tools I use. What I want to explore next is how these areas can come together. I am interested in using code, data, design, and storytelling to create experiences that do more than just look good. I want to understand how digital tools can change the way people interact with information and with each other. For me, that is becoming a much bigger part of what digital literacy means.
